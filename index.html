How to read this number
Twenty readings are each mapped to a 0-100 risk value and averaged by weight. Low scores mean the factors agree that conditions are calm and trending; high scores mean they agree that they are not. Middling scores usually mean disagreement between blocks, which the block bars above will show.

Under 25, conditions are as supportive as this model measures: price above both averages, quiet volatility, spreads tight, breadth intact. 25 to 45 is the ordinary state of a market that is working with some rough edges. 45 to 62 means several blocks have turned at once. Above 62 is the region occupied by corrections and credit events, where the same signals that flag danger also mark the prices that later look cheap.

The percentile line matters more than the level. A 31 that ranks in the 70th percentile of the last three years is a different message than a 31 in the 20th, because the mapping thresholds are fixed while regimes are not.
What it does not tell you
Nothing about direction, size or timing. The score is a summary of conditions that have already happened, built from closing data, so it lags any move now in progress and cannot see a gap tomorrow morning.

The thresholds and weights are hand-set judgment, not fitted to anything, and they were chosen with the whole sample visible. The sample table below inherits that, so treat it as a description of the past rather than evidence of an edge. A high-risk reading is also what a market bottom looks like from the inside, and a low-risk reading is what the top of a long expansion looks like, which is why this measures conditions rather than opportunity.

It carries no view on your position size, time horizon, cost basis or tax situation, and it is not investment advice. Two people can read the same 31 and correctly do opposite things.
Blocks, weights and missing data
Trend carries 25 points, volatility 25, credit and macro 20, momentum 15, internals 15. Any factor whose data is missing is dropped and the rest are renormalized, so a day scored on 14 factors is still on the same 0-100 scale but is a coarser reading. The factor table shows which ones are absent, and the score is withheld entirely if less than 55 points of weight survive.

How each reading becomes a risk number
Reading is the raw market value. Risk is that value mapped to 0-100 through a fixed curve, so the two move in opposite directions for most factors: a wide gap above the 200d average produces a low risk chip, a spike in credit spreads produces a high one. Two factors are deliberately not one-directional. RSI is penalized below 40 and above 78, because both a breakdown and an overbought push are poor moments to add. The 21d change works the same way past about 9 percent.

Percentile factors compare today against the last three years of its own history rather than a fixed level, which keeps VIX, realized volatility and credit spreads meaningful across regimes. Weight is the share of the composite each factor carries.

The 5d and 20d columns are the change in that factor's risk number over those windows, in risk points, so a red +12 means the factor deteriorated by 12 points while a green -8 means it improved. Changes under 1.5 points read as flat. The sparkline is the same risk number over the last six months on a fixed 0 to 100 scale, with the midline at 50, so a line drifting up is a factor steadily getting worse regardless of where it sits today. A factor can hold a low risk chip while trending sharply upward, which the single number cannot show.

Macro series are aligned to their publication date rather than their observation date: high yield spreads and the 2s10s curve are lagged one day, the financial conditions index six, since FRED stamps each value with the date it describes and releases it later. Without that shift the history would use numbers that were not knowable at the time.

Reading the rank correlation table
Each row is the Spearman rank correlation between that factor's risk number on a given day and what SPY did over the following window. Negative is the direction the model claims: higher risk preceded weaker returns. Positive means the factor pointed the wrong way over that sample.

The noise band printed above the table is roughly two standard errors under the optimistic assumption of independent observations. Correlations inside it are indistinguishable from zero. Because forward windows overlap, the effective sample is closer to the day count divided by the horizon, so the 63 day column rests on well under a hundred independent observations and its band is wide.

The half columns matter more than the full column. A factor that holds the same sign and rough magnitude in both halves is at least stable across two regimes. One that flips sign is telling you the full-sample number came from a single episode.
What this backtest cannot establish
Every threshold, weight and mapping curve in this model was set by hand with the whole sample already visible. That makes all of it in-sample, including the halves, since the design was not frozen before seeing the second half. Treat a good result as a failure to disprove rather than as evidence.

The rule is deliberately crude: fully long below the threshold, flat above it, taking position from the prior close and earning the next day's return. It charges no commission, no slippage, no bid-ask, no taxes and no interest on the flat periods, all of which favor the rule. Ten years covers one deep drawdown and one long expansion, so the result largely reflects how the threshold happened to sit around a handful of episodes.

The comparison that usually holds up is drawdown, not return. Sitting out high-volatility stretches typically costs compounding and buys a smaller worst case, and a rule that appears to beat buy and hold on both is more likely overfit than good. Check the switch count too: a rule that trades constantly is reacting to noise in the score.

Risk at price peaks and troughs

How the pivots are found and why this is not a signal
A close is marked a peak when it is the highest close within the chosen number of days on both sides, and a trough when it is the lowest. Consecutive pivots of the same kind collapse to the more extreme one, and swings smaller than the minimum are discarded, which is what keeps a choppy sideways stretch from producing dozens of meaningless pivots.

Every pivot is defined using days that came after it, so none of them were knowable at the time. The most recent stretch equal to the pivot strength can never contain a pivot for the same reason, and the newest pivot shown may still be revised by what happens next week. Nothing in this panel can be traded.

Expect high scores at troughs and low scores at peaks. That is the model working as designed rather than a discovery: volatility, drawdown and credit spreads all reach their worst readings at price lows. The practical consequence is the uncomfortable one. A rule that only buys when this score is low will buy closer to peaks than to troughs, which is the cost of a conditions gauge and the reason the evaluation card compares drawdown rather than return.

The scatter is the useful half. If score at a pivot carried information about the size of the move that followed, the cloud would slope. Two years gives roughly ten to twenty pivots, so a correlation here needs to be very large before it means anything, and the rank figure is printed with its count for that reason.
